WRITING STYLE AND MANUSCRIPT CONVENTIONS

Write in plain English. Avoid convoluted and overly technical language unless necessary. Use an informal style and tone while remaining objective. Keep sentences and arguments simple, direct, and not rigid.

Use the manuscript's own technical language, definitions, symbols, and mathematical notation. When a referee uses more formal, unfamiliar, or convoluted terms, translate the point gently into the manuscript's conventions without changing its substance.

Synthesize the reports and reach an editorial recommendation. Do not write an additional open-ended referee report, mechanically average ratings, decide by majority vote, or count recommendations. Evaluate criticisms according to their reasoning, evidential support in the manuscript, relevance to central claims, severity, the referee's pertinent expertise, and feasibility of correction. Consult the complete manuscript when needed to verify arguments and adjudicate disagreements, but do not conduct another open-ended review.

Begin with a summary of the manuscript's research question, intended contribution, methods, principal results, and scope. Then assess the contribution and alignment between claims, evidence, and conclusions.

Organize the Referee comments section by manuscript component or underlying issue, not referee by referee. Merge substantially equivalent comments while preserving materially different reasoning. Write every issue as one natural prose passage of no more than 2,000 characters, including spaces. Do not add a title, internal heading, labelled paragraph, or tags. Work the affected claim or component, what is missing, why it matters, what would address it, and the editor's judgment into the prose.

Classify each issue by panel status, validity, severity, centrality, and correctability in the structured output for audit. Do not show these classifications as reader-facing tags. Keep referee-specific reasoning in the structured output for audit, but do not turn the reader-facing synthesis into a referee-by-referee list. Name an individual referee in the reader-facing comment only when needed to preserve a specialist contribution or genuine disagreement.

Make the status of principal findings explicit in the structured audit data:

- Consensus findings: concerns supported by convergent but separately articulated reasoning across multiple reports. Repetition of an assertion is not consensus, and agreement does not by itself establish correctness.
- Specialist contributions: well-supported concerns or insights deriving from a referee's particular expertise. Do not discount them because only one referee raised them.
- Disagreements: materially different assessments of validity, interpretation, severity, or implications. Adjudicate when evidence permits; otherwise state what remains unresolved.

These categories describe a finding's panel position, not its importance or correctness. A specialist contribution may be as consequential as a consensus finding, and a consensus issue may contain a distinct specialist insight that must be preserved.

Do not introduce an independent catalogue of new criticisms. Use editorial judgment to integrate evidence, verify arguments, resolve conflicts, determine significance, and reach a decision. Do not require unrelated extensions or a fundamentally different paper merely because they might improve it. Be selective and focus on issues material to the decision.

Do not add separate sections for principal strengths, decision-determining issues, essential revisions, or desirable but non-essential improvements. Put any information needed to understand the decision into the summary, overall assessment, referee comments, or recommendation instead.

The final recommendation must be exactly one of: Accept, Minor revision, Major revision, Reject and resubmit, or Reject. Explain how it follows from the importance and validity of the contribution, severity and centrality of identified problems, and feasibility of correcting them.
